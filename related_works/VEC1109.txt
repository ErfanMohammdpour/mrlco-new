Mobility-Aware Multi-hop Task Offloading for
Autonomous Driving in Vehicular Edge Computing
and Networks
Lei Liu, Ming Zhao, Miao Yu, Mian Ahmad Jan, Dapeng Lan and Amirhosein Taherkordi
Abstract—Vehicular Edge Computing (VEC) has gained sig-
nificant interest due to its potential to provide low latency and
reduce the load in backhaul networks. To meet the increasing
computational demands of emerging and ever-growing vehicular
applications, e.g. autonomous driving, abundance in computation
resources of individual vehicles can play a crucial role in task
execution within a VEC scenario, that can further contribute
towards improving the user experience. This is however an
extremely challenging task due to high mobility of vehicles that
can easily lead to intermittent connectivity, thereby disrupting
on-going task processing. In this paper, we propose a task
offloading scheme by exploiting multi-hop vehicle computation
resources in VEC based on mobility analysis of vehicles. In
addition to the vehicles within one hop from the task vehicle
that generates computation tasks, certain multi-hop vehicles
that meet the given requirements in terms of link connectivity
and computation capacity, are also leveraged to carry out the
tasks offloaded by the task vehicle. An optimization problem is
formulated for the task vehicle to minimize the weighted sum of
execution time and computation cost of all tasks. A semidefinite
relaxation approach with an adaptive adjustment procedure
is proposed to solve the formulated optimization problem for
obtaining the corresponding offloading decisions. The simulation
results show that our proposed offloading scheme can achieve
significant improvement in terms of response delay by at least
34% compared with the existing algorithms, i.e., local processing
and random offloading.
Index Terms—Vehicular edge computing, task offloading,
multi-hop, mobility, connectivity.

safety, enhancing traffic efficiency and providing convenient
infotainment services to the users [9]. Nonetheless, such

(VEC) is viewed as a promising approach [11], [12]. With the
integration of Mobile Edge Computing (MEC) [13]–[15] and
vehicular networks, VEC aims at reducing the response time
and alleviating backhaul network load by moving the computa-
tion and storage resources in proximity to vehicular users [16].
However, due to unique characteristics of vehicular networks
[17], especially the high mobility of nodes and varying channel
conditions, designing an efficient edge-enabled task offloading
scheme is quite challenging.
There have been numerous efforts devoted to task offload-
ing in VEC, most of which focus on investigating server-
based offloading strategy where Road Side Units (RSUs) with
certain communication, computation and storage resources
mainly act as edge servers. In [18], workload scheduling
was implemented on a single edge server. The work in [19]
considered a multi-server network scenario. In [20] and [21],
mobility-based task offloading among multiple edge servers
was studied. However, the penetration of edge servers is low at
initial stages of deployment. Besides, the deployment of edge
servers can be quite expensive. From a business perspective,
it is impractical to fully deploy edge servers along the road
segments, especially in a large-scale network. Edge servers, on
the other hand, struggle to keep up with rapidly growing traffic
data due to restricted computation capability when compared
to the cloud.
Vehicle-to-Vehicle (V2V) offloading is an exciting solution
for dealing with the limitations of server-based offloading and
mitigating the scarcity of processing resources in VEC. The
increasing growth in the processing speed and storage capacity
allow the smart and connected vehicles to communicate as well
as permit them to have some computation and cache resources
for task processing. The presence of large underutilized re-
sources in individual vehicles presents a huge opportunity for
improving vehicular networks’ communication and processing
capability. The aggregation of these abundant idle resources
can improve resource utilization and user experience. All these
factors provide a powerful platform for exploiting the vehicle
resources. V2V offloading, in particular, benefits from being
close to vehicular users, allowing proximity services to be
provided through resource sharing.
In literature, there has been few studies conducted for
designing an efficient V2V offloading schemes. Some of these

studies make use of the computation resources of vehicles for
service provisioning, but fail to fully capture the mobility of
vehicles and are based on the assumption that all the vehicles
are willing to share resources for free, e.g, [22]. Specifically,
most of existing work on V2V offloading mainly depend on
one-hop vehicles for task execution, ignoring the utilization of
available multi-hop vehicles. Based on this statement, there is
an urgent need to design an efficient V2V offloading scheme.
To this end, the following factors should be well considered
for any such design:
1) Resource discovery. The mobility and computational ca-
pacity for each vehicle are different. Multi-hop link, on
the other hand, is vulnerable because of the complicated
channel environment and dynamic changes in topology.
Therefore, it is crucial to find available vehicles for task
processing.
2) Response latency. Due to the computational and trans-
mission needs, response delay is regarded as the most
demanding metric to measure the quality of service.
Thus, minimizing the response delay is one of the main
targets for an offloading scheme.
3) Incentive scheme. Considering the selfishness of nodes,
the owners of computation and storage resources may
not be willing to rent out their resources unless they get
paid well. An incentive strategy is therefore necessary
to encourage resource sharing among the nodes.
4) Mobility. Suffering from the high mobility of nodes, the
network topology varies with time, which introduces
difficulties in providing optimal decision making. In
addition, due to the short contact time between nodes,
a successful offloading process should guarantee that
the task offloaded from a task vehicle to its service
vehicle should be completed before the link between
them becomes broken.
To address these issues, we propose a Mobility-Aware
Multi-hop Task Offloading (MMTO) in vehicular edge com-
puting, with the aim to maximize resource utilization and
enhance user experience by making the best use of the
underutilized computation resources of vehicles. Its novelty
is that the offloading policy is constructed by addressing the
difficulties in task assignment caused by mobility for one-hop
service vehicles and taking advantage of multi-hop service
vehicles for task computation. For one-hop service vehicles,
the assigned tasks need to be finished before they move
out of the transmission range of the task vehicle. As for
multi-hop service vehicles, they are capable of participating
in task processing only if the link connectivity between the
task vehicle and the service vehicle is available before the
calculation results are returned to the task vehicle. Based
on the proposed task offloading scheme, a mixed-integer
program is formulated. To provide a solution to this problem,
a semidefinite relaxation approach is proposed along with an
adaptive adjustment procedure considering the connectivity
constraints on one-hop and multi-hop service vehicles. The
contributions of our work are summarized as follows.
• A task offloading scheme is proposed by utilizing multi-
hop vehicles which meet the given requirements regard-
ing mobility pattern and computation capacity, in addition
to one hop vehicles of the task vehicle.
• An offloading policy is designed considering the response
delay and economic cost to minimize the utility of task
vehicle, subject to the connectivity constraints.
• A semidefinite relaxation (SDR) approach with an adap-
tive adjustment scheme is presented to solve the formu-
lated optimization problem by considering the connectiv-
ity constraints of one-hop and multi-hop service vehicles
while offloading the tasks.

II. RELATED WORK
In literature, a number of studies have been devoted to the
research on vehicle edge computing (VEC), some solid works
among which are introduced in this section.
In VEC, edge servers with certain computation and cache
resources, e.g., RSUs, are mainly used for vehicular users to
execute certain tasks. The work in [18] investigated work-
load offloading and task scheduling by accounting for the
high mobility of vehicles and their sojourn time within the
communication range of edge servers. In [23], a dual-side
optimization problem was formulated with the aim to bal-
ance the interests of both vehicles and edge servers at the
same time. The computation offloading behavior of different
vehicles is regarded as a competition for an edge server’s
resources in [24], where vehicles are to allowed to adjust
their offloading probabilities in the pursuit of maximizing the
utility. The works in [18], [23], [24] mainly focused on a
single edge server. On the other hand, the works of [19],
[25]–[27] focused on multi-server scenarios. An architecture
for vehicle-infrastructure communication with high reliability
and low latency was proposed in [19], which optimizes the
association of vehicle and a small base station along with
wireless resource management. In [25], the designed task
offloading scheme simultaneously optimizes the selection of
target server and the determination of transmission model.
Communication, caching and computation are jointly taken
into account to reduce the delay in [26]. Similar to [26], the
authors utilized a deep reinforcement learning approach to
solve the resource allocation problem with the consideration
of networking, caching and computation in [27]. The work
of [28] aimed at the joint optimization of task offloading and
resource management while taking into account the differential
privacy as well.
The popularization and intelligence of vehicles make it
possible to fully exploit idle vehicle computation and cache
resources for service provisioning. Owing to the remark-
able characteristics including large number of vehicles, long
sojourn time as well as specific staying locations, parked
vehicles are regarded as a good choice to serve as service
infrastructures. Clusters of parked vehicles were used to

are exploited for providing computation services. With the
aim to minimize the average offloading delay, an adaptive

The delay performance of one-hop service vehicles is learnt by
the task vehicle in a distributed manner. A V2V task offloading
framework was presented in [33], where the computation re-
sources of available surrounding vehicles can be utilized by the
task vehicle. Specially, context-aware opportunistic offloading
algorithms are implemented with the consideration of vehicle
speed, direction and location to deal with mobility issues. In
[34], a k-hop-limited V2I vehicular data offloading scheme
was designed. The novelty of this scheme lies in finding the
multi-hop V2V path by which the front or the rear RSU can
be connected from the task vehicle, thereby increasing the
possibility of vehicular data offloading. The novelty of the
designed task offloading strategy in [35] lies in combining the
dual features of V2V, i.e., communication and sensing. The
scheduling policy, bandwidth ratio and transmission choice
are jointly optimized for the purpose of minimizing the total
response latency.
In VEC, the high mobility of vehicles should be well
addressed, which will impact the success of task offloading.
When a task is offloaded by a task vehicle to an edge
server, it is likely that the task vehicle needs to pass through
multiple edge servers before the task is finished. In order
to deal with this issue, a mobility-based task offloading is
agreed as a promising approach [20] [21]. A prediction-based
transmission scheme was proposed in [20] for task processing
with the consideration of mobility of vehicles, which enables
the improvement of transmission efficiency while meeting the
delay requirement. The work in [21] studies the problem
of resource allocation in a multi-user multi-server network
scenario by integrating load balancing with task offloading.
Each vehicle can offload its task to a desired server for
computation through movement. In [36], a generic relative
motion model was provided to design a link stability metric
by which the most stable vehicles surrounding the task vehicle
are discovered. Among these stable vehicles, the vehicle which
meets the task vehicle’s preferences and the service constraints
is selected as the optimal service provider. The authors in [37]
designed a mobility-oriented retrieval scheme, where vehicles
and RSUs are used for the communication nodes. In this
scheme, the processed data can be effectively retrieved. An
efficient mobility-based task offloading scheme was investi-
gated in [38], which aims at the selection of offloading time

and the allocation of communication, computing and caching
resources with the consideration of the vehicle mobility and
the latency constraint. In [39], the random mobility of vehicles
and the potential handover during the offloading process
are considered while designing a mobility-aware offloading
algorithm.

in Table I. In summary, most of the existing works on
task offloading in VEC are assisted by edge servers. While
mobility has been viewed as a challenge, we introduce a new
perspective that enables us to exploit the link connectivity for
optimizing task offloading further. In particular, we include
multi-hop vehicles of the task vehicle in addition to one-hop
vehicles for task offloading. In this regard, our work is one
of the first to explore underutilized computation resources
in individual vehicles to enhance the edge capacity by con-
sidering the potential opportunity of multi-hop vehicles on
task implementation, which is beneficial for guaranteeing the
quality of service of vehicular users.
III. SYSTEM OVERVIEW
Our work focuses on V2V offloading as illustrated in Fig.1.
The considered scenario is a straight road, e.g., a highway,
where vehicles are moving in two different directions. The
main symbols used in this paper are summarized in Table II.
In the proposed scenario, there are two types of vehicles,
i.e., client vehicles and service vehicles. They are classified
based on their roles in task processing. Vehicles which gener-
ate tasks and need to process these tasks are defined as client
vehicles, while vehicles which have surplus idle computation
resources and are willing to share their resources are defined as
service vehicles. Note that the role of each vehicle can change
with time, which is dependent on whether it has a task to be
executed or has abundant computation resources to share.
A vehicular application may consist of multiple services.
For example, AR-based driving assistance application is com-
posed of object recognition and video streaming as introduced
in [40]. Each service has its attributes, including data size,
computation complexity, etc. Here, we regard each service
as a task. For a client vehicle, its tasks can be processed by
the following two strategies: (a) Computed locally: the client
vehicle processes its tasks by itself. (b) Offloaded to nearby
vehicles: the client vehicle uploads its tasks to its neighboring
vehicles with surplus computation resources for task execution.

The general process of task processing includes: discover
service vehicles, allocate client vehicle’s tasks and implement
computation tasks, which are described in detail as below.
1) Service Vehicle Discovery: In vehicular networks, vehi-
cles exchange the speed and position information with their
neighbors by periodically broadcasting beacons. For service
vehicles, they can announce their availability by adding the
computation information in the sent beacons. When a client
vehicle needs to process the generated tasks, it listens to the
beacons from its neighboring vehicles to find some available
service vehicles for task offloading.
(a) One-hop service vehicles: For a client vehicle, vehicles
within its one-hop range are called as one-hop vehicles, which
can act as candidate service vehicle. This is because the client
vehicle can directly communicate with each one-hop vehicle,
which enables the task offloading between them. As shown in
Fig. 2, vehicle 2 and vehicle 3 are within the communication
range of client vehicle 1. In this case, vehicle 1 can directly
upload its tasks to vehicle 2 and vehicle 3 for processing if
needed.

(b) Multi-hop service vehicles: Vehicles within the multi-
hop range of the client vehicle are also potential service
vehicles. This is based on the fact that these multi-hop
vehicles can connect to the client vehicle with the multi-
hop transmission of intermediate vehicles. At the initial stage,
the client vehicle cannot directly communicate with its multi-
hop vehicles due to the limitation of inter-vehicle distance.
However, it can obtain the information (e.g., speed, position
and computation resources) of its multi-hop vehicles assisted
by its intermediate vehicles which can serve as relay nodes.
The client vehicle broadcasts the service requirements for
task execution (e.g., delay and energy consumption). After
receiving this information, each one-hop vehicle will continue
to transmit it onward when the link connectivity satisfies
certain conditions. On the other hand, the one-hop vehicle
can reply the client vehicle for being service vehicle along
the reverse path if it fulfills the service requirements. This
procedure is repeated until reaching the maximal hop number.
Given a service vehicle, more than one routing paths may exist
from itself to the client vehicle. The routing path with best
transmission performance can be determined as the optimal
one for data transmission. Based on the collected information
related to multi-hop vehicles, the client vehicle is able to judge
if these multi-hop vehicles are available. If a multi-hop vehicle
is selected as the service vehicle to help the client vehicle in
task processing, it first receives the offloaded tasks from the
client vehicle with the assistance of intermediate relay nodes
and then participates in task processing. For example, vehicle
1 broadcasts the offloaded tasks in Fig. 2. Upon the reception
of these tasks, one-hop vehicle 2 and one-hop vehicle 3 are
responsible for relaying these tasks to two-hop vehicles 4 and
5, respectively. The selected two-hop service vehicle executes
the assigned tasks and then transmits the calculated results to
the client vehicle using a multi-hop transmission.
2) Task Allocation of Client Vehicles: Upon collecting the
information of available service vehicles, the client vehicle
needs to design an efficient task allocation algorithm to decide
where and how to run its tasks to meet its requirements.
(a) The design of optimization problem: In order to make
the optimal offloading decisions, an optimization problem
is formulated where the processing delay and the incentive
pay are considered. The delay includes the uploading delay
by wireless transmission, computing delay at vehicles, and
downloading delay through wireless transmission. Different
from traditional MEC, the total delay in VEC is also affected
by the inter-vehicle distance between the client vehicle and
its service vehicles. With the aim to improve the utilization
of vehicular resources and the experience of vehicular users,
an incentive scheme is necessary to encourage more vehicles
with abundant idle computation resources to participate in
task offloading. For the client vehicle, it needs to take into
account the payment when resorting to service vehicles for
task processing while satisfying the applicable requirements.
(b) Constraints: The link connectivity plays a crucial role in
guaranteeing task offloading. Due to the mobility of nodes, fre-
quent link disruptions are unavoidable in vehicular networks.
Thus, the link connectivity between the client vehicle and its
one-hop service vehicles will considerably impact the task
allocation. When the client vehicle offloads the tasks to its
one-hop service vehicles for task processing, the better the link
connectivity, the larger the successful offloading probability
will be. As shown in Fig. 3, a client vehicle A uploads some

tasks to its one-hop vehicle C. However, as the short contact

The link connectivity should also be considered for multi-
hop service vehicles. Different from the link between the
client and its one-hop service vehicle, the one between the
client and its multi-hop service vehicle is more likely to be
interrupted. An available multi-hop service vehicle can obtain
the assigned tasks from the client vehicle through multi-hop
wireless transmission, and has the chance to send the result
to the client vehicle if the link between the client vehicle and
the multi-hop service vehicle remains connected during the
offloading process.
3) The Implementation of Computation Tasks: By solving
the formulated problem, the client vehicle can obtain the op-
timal offloading decisions. Based on the offloading decisions,
if it is necessary to depend on some service vehicles for
task processing, the client vehicle transmits its tasks to its
service vehicles. Once these task are received, each service
vehicle begins to participate in task processing. After finishing
the assigned tasks, service vehicles send the results back.
Receiving all the results, the client vehicle can successfully run
its application. The entire flow chart of the proposed offloading
scheme above is illustrated in Fig.4.

a local optimum solution to problem P1 by minimizing the

follows:
• Make the list of all N tasks from the client vehicle with
random order.
• According to this list, examine each task in sequence.

decisions. We check each feasible offloading decision of
task i without changing the offloading decisions of the
other tasks. For any feasible offloading decision of task
i, calculate the corresponding utility of this client vehicle.

decision, update the solution xini and exit this iteration.
If i is equal to N, stop this procedure.
• Repeat this procedure until xini is converged to a solution.
Because the number of available options in xini is limited,
the iteration will converge to a solution eventually.

VI. CONCLUSION
In this paper, we focused on the design of vehicle-to-vehicle
offloading strategy to improve the resource utilization and
enhance user experience. The problem of exploiting mobility
task offloading in VEC networks was studied to minimize the
utility of client vehicles, which was measured by a weighted
sum of execution time and computation cost. Different from
existing works, the offloading decisions were made by taking
into account both the challenge and the opportunity brought
by the mobility of vehicles. Besides one-hop vehicles, two-
hop vehicles of client vehicles were employed to facilitate task
processing when feasible. A semidefinite relaxation approach
was utilized along with an adaptive adjustment algorithm to
